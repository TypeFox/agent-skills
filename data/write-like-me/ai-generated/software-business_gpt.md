# Report: How the Software Development Business Is Changing

## Executive summary

The software development business is not contracting in the sense that companies need less software. The opposite is true: worldwide software spending is still growing quickly, AI investment is accelerating, cloud infrastructure continues expanding, and demand for software engineers remains structurally strong. Gartner’s July 2026 forecast puts worldwide IT spending at $6.37 trillion for 2026, up 14.2%, with software spending growing 15.5% and IaaS 29.3%.

What **is** changing rapidly is **where the economic value sits in software development**.

For roughly two decades, a large part of the software services industry scaled through a relatively straightforward model: more projects meant more developers, more developer-days, larger delivery organizations, and frequently labor arbitrage. AI-assisted development, cloud platforms, mature open-source ecosystems, low-code tools, and increasingly capable standard software are reducing the amount of undifferentiated implementation work required to create software. At the same time, integration complexity, legacy modernization, security obligations, domain requirements, and the need to turn AI prototypes into reliable systems are increasing.

The resulting market is becoming polarized. Commodity implementation is under increasing pressure. Specialized engineering, architecture, modernization, domain expertise, platform engineering, AI integration, security, and responsibility for business outcomes are becoming more valuable.

This distinction already appears in services-market data. ISG reports that overall managed-services annual contract value grew only 1.3% in 2025, while its engineering-services category grew **35%**, with average engineering R&D deal value rising 21%.  The interesting story is therefore not "software services are disappearing." It is that **generic capacity and differentiated engineering are diverging**.

| Structural shift      | Previous center of gravity      | Emerging center of gravity                            |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Development           | Writing implementation code     | Defining, directing and verifying systems             |
| External services     | Additional engineering capacity | Scarce expertise and responsibility                   |
| Commercial model      | Hours/FTEs                      | Fixed scope, assets, hybrid and outcome models        |
| Competitive advantage | Number of developers            | Expertise, IP, domain knowledge and leverage          |
| AI                    | Developer productivity tool     | Part of the engineering operating model               |
| Architecture          | Project concern                 | Productivity multiplier and AI prerequisite           |
| Open source           | Free building blocks            | Strategic infrastructure requiring governance         |
| Security              | Specialist downstream activity  | Design and supply-chain requirement                   |
| Buying                | Build versus buy                | Buy + build + integrate                               |
| Talent                | Broad developer growth          | Higher leverage from experienced, AI-fluent engineers |

The strongest conclusion from the research is this:

**Software is becoming cheaper to produce at the code level, but not necessarily cheaper to engineer, integrate, own and evolve.**

That distinction will reshape the economics of development companies over the remainder of the decade.

## 1. Demand for software remains strong, but spending is being reallocated

There is little evidence that businesses are deciding software itself matters less. Gartner's latest 2026 forecast expects software spending to reach about **$1.47 trillion**, up 15.5% year over year. Infrastructure-as-a-Service is expected to rise almost 30%. Services grow more slowly, at 5.3%, which is itself revealing: money is flowing disproportionately toward software, AI infrastructure and cloud capacity rather than traditional labor-intensive services. Gartner explicitly cautions that this is not a market in which every technology supplier benefits equally because AI, infrastructure costs and other priorities are competing for constrained budgets.

ISG finds a similar split. Its 2025 global market data shows relatively modest growth in traditional IT outsourcing, including only slight gains in application development and maintenance, while engineering services grew substantially faster. 

AI is also actively cannibalizing other IT priorities rather than simply adding an unlimited new budget. An ISG survey of 200 organizations found AI initiatives represented the largest expected increase in 2026 IT budgets, ahead of security, applications and cloud platforms. 

This means the attractive question for a development business is increasingly not **"Will customers continue buying software development?"** but **"Which kinds of software development will continue commanding premium budgets?"**

The evidence points toward work connected directly to differentiation, intellectual property, complex engineering, modernization, AI, security and specialized domains.

## 2. AI has crossed from experimentation into everyday software engineering

The speed of adoption is extraordinary.

Stack Overflow's 2025 survey found **84% of respondents either use or plan to use AI tools in development**, and 51% of professional developers use them daily. DORA's 2025 research reports approximately 90% AI adoption among its respondents. GitHub recorded more than 180 million developers on its platform in 2025, with nearly 80% of new developers using Copilot during their first week.

This matters commercially because AI assistance is becoming less of a competitive advantage in itself. If essentially every engineering organization has access to similar foundation models and coding agents, saying "our engineers use AI" will soon be equivalent to saying "our engineers use an IDE."

The important differentiation becomes **how effectively the organization structures work around AI**.

And the productivity evidence is much more nuanced than marketing claims suggest. A randomized Google study involving 96 software engineers estimated an approximately **21% reduction in time** for a complex enterprise development task when developers used AI. Conversely, METR studied experienced open-source developers working in codebases they knew well and found early-2025 AI tools made them **19% slower**.

Those results are not necessarily contradictory. AI performs very differently depending on the task, codebase, developer expertise, available context and surrounding engineering system.

Stack Overflow's data reinforces that interpretation. Although adoption is high, **46% of developers distrust AI output accuracy compared with 33% who trust it**. The most common frustration, reported by 66%, is solutions that are "almost right"; 45% report that debugging AI-generated code can take longer. And despite enormous attention around autonomous agents, a majority still either do not use agents or use simpler AI tools.

So the near-term disruption is not autonomous AI replacing software companies. It is **AI changing the unit economics of particular engineering activities**.

## 3. The developer's role is moving upward in abstraction

GitHub's research with advanced AI users provides an interesting qualitative picture of where this leads.

Its 2025 interviews found highly AI-fluent developers spending less time directly producing code and more time defining intent, decomposing problems, supplying context, establishing constraints, directing agents and verifying results. GitHub describes the center of gravity moving toward architecture, orchestration and verification. It explicitly notes that architecture becomes *more*, rather than less, important when AI produces larger portions of implementation code.

This is likely to be one of the most important business consequences of AI.

A conventional project might once have required one architect, several experienced engineers and several developers implementing fairly well-defined functionality. If AI removes a substantial share of the routine implementation, the economically efficient team may become smaller and more senior.

But the remaining work is harder to commoditize: understanding the customer's domain, making architectural decisions, translating vague requirements into precise specifications, integrating existing systems, evaluating tradeoffs, reviewing generated code, testing behavior and taking responsibility for the result.

In other words, **AI attacks the lowest-value part of many development engagements first**.

That makes expertise more—not less—important to service differentiation.

## 4. The labor market shows the same split

Employment statistics do not support a simple "AI eliminates programmers" narrative, but they do suggest that the nature of software employment is changing.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects employment of software developers to grow **16% from 2024 to 2034**, substantially faster than overall employment. At the same time, the narrower occupation "computer programmer" is projected to **decline 6%**, with BLS specifically citing automation of repetitive programming activities.

That distinction is remarkably consistent with the wider evidence: **programming is becoming easier to automate while software engineering remains valuable**.

Europe shows continuing structural demand as well. Eurostat reports 10.45 million ICT specialists in the EU in 2025, representing 5% of employment. The number has increased about 59% since 2015.

Skills nevertheless remain scarce. Among EU companies that attempted to recruit ICT specialists, 57.5% reported difficulty filling those positions; in Germany the figure exceeded 72%.

The post-pandemic hiring boom has clearly ended, however. LinkedIn's 2026 analysis describes historically weak U.S. software-engineering hiring following the sharp 2022–2023 slowdown. It finds entry paths tightening while AI-related roles expand rapidly: the share of hires classified as AI engineers in 2025 was **14 times its 2019 level**. LinkedIn stresses that much of the broader hiring weakness remains macroeconomic rather than proven AI displacement. 

This creates an unusual market: fewer organizations are indiscriminately adding developers, yet valuable software expertise remains difficult to obtain.

That favors businesses selling **capability rather than headcount**.

## 5. Outsourcing is becoming much more selective

Perhaps the most significant change for software service companies comes from Deloitte's 2024 Global Outsourcing Survey.

The superficially contradictory findings tell the story well. **70% of surveyed executives said their organizations had selectively brought previously outsourced work back in-house during the previous five years**, and 78% were using global in-house centers. Yet **80% planned to maintain or increase third-party outsourcing investment**. Half were already outsourcing areas that include front-office activities and R&D rather than merely traditional back-office functions.

Customers therefore are not simply choosing between "internal development" and "outsourcing."

They increasingly operate a portfolio of internal engineering teams, global capability centers, software suppliers, cloud/SaaS vendors, specialists, contractors and AI systems.

This substantially weakens the old proposition:

> We can provide five Java developers for twelve months.

A client with strong internal engineering capability, access to international recruiting and increasingly productive AI tooling has many substitutes for generic capacity.

A much stronger proposition becomes:

> We understand this problem unusually well, can solve the difficult part faster than your internal organization, and leave you with a maintainable capability.

Deloitte explicitly says skilled talent and agility now join cost reduction as major outsourcing drivers and describes a movement toward value-oriented supplier relationships.

That creates a particularly favorable environment for smaller specialist companies—provided their differentiation is real.

## 6. Pricing is starting to decouple from developer hours

AI presents an uncomfortable economic problem for companies built around time-and-materials billing.

Suppose a team previously required 1,000 hours for a project and AI eventually allows it to deliver an equivalent result in 600. If the company simply bills 600 hours, the customer captures virtually all of the productivity improvement while the service provider reduces its revenue by 40%.

That is sustainable only if the provider can compensate through greater volume—which pushes the company back toward a scale business.

The market is consequently experimenting with alternatives. Forrester surveyed more than 2,300 enterprise service decision-makers and found **45% expected to increase their use of performance-based pricing and 46% expected greater use of fixed-price contracts**. Forrester argues that service providers increasingly need to act as co-innovation partners and bring reusable software, data, IP and delivery assets rather than relying exclusively on people.

This does **not** mean time-and-materials contracts disappear. Complex engineering frequently involves uncertainty that neither supplier nor customer can responsibly price in advance. Nor is pure outcome pricing easy when business outcomes depend on factors outside the supplier's control.

The more plausible development is a mixed market: T&M for genuinely exploratory work, fixed pricing where AI and reusable technology make delivery predictable, subscriptions or licenses around reusable assets, and hybrid models that connect part of the compensation to measurable results.

The central commercial change is therefore **the weakening relationship between effort and value**.

## 7. AI creates a second software-development market: helping enterprises use AI safely

AI affects software businesses not only by changing how they deliver projects. It is itself creating a major new category of development work.

Accenture illustrates the magnitude of that shift. Its FY2025 generative and agentic AI revenue reached **$2.7 billion**, three times the previous year, while related bookings reached **$5.9 billion**.

But buyers are moving beyond generic chatbot experiments. The harder work involves connecting models to enterprise data and tools, designing reliable context, encoding business rules, establishing permissions, evaluating outputs, handling failure, observing agents and incorporating human approval.

DORA's 2025 findings are particularly important here. It describes AI as an **amplifier**: organizations with strong platforms, feedback loops and engineering practices benefit, while organizations with poor foundations can simply create more output and more downstream problems. Around 90% of organizations in its survey had adopted an internal platform, and 76% reported dedicated platform teams.

The high-value AI engineering business therefore moves quickly beyond model selection and prompting. It becomes conventional—but demanding—systems engineering around probabilistic components.

That is a substantial opportunity for development companies that understand both AI and traditional software engineering.

## 8. Platform engineering and developer tooling are becoming strategic infrastructure

The increasing speed of code production creates another constraint: organizational throughput.

If AI lets each developer generate more code, bottlenecks move into builds, tests, deployments, reviews, architecture, documentation, compliance and operations. A poor development environment can absorb much of the nominal productivity improvement.

That explains the extraordinary rise of platform engineering. DORA reports that 90% of surveyed organizations have adopted at least one internal platform. HashiCorp separately found 95% of surveyed organizations had at least begun establishing a platform team, although fewer than half had fully standardized cloud operations.

Cloud-native development itself is now mainstream infrastructure rather than an emerging practice. CNCF reported 15.6 million cloud-native developers in 2025 and found that 77% of backend developers used at least one cloud-native technology.

The strategic implication is subtle: as code generation becomes inexpensive, **the environments that constrain, validate, build, integrate and operate that code become more valuable**.

Developer platforms, specialized IDEs, domain-specific tools, language tooling, test infrastructure and automated validation can therefore capture increasing value even while individual coding activities become cheaper.

## 9. "Build versus buy" is turning into "buy, build and blend"

Enterprise application strategy has historically been discussed as a binary choice: buy standard software or build something custom.

That model is increasingly obsolete.

Gartner now explicitly describes a **"buy, build and blend"** strategy in which organizations combine purchased applications, internally developed functionality and integrations.

Low-code platforms reinforce this development. Gartner expects the low-code development technology market to reach $58.2 billion in 2029 and identifies agentic AI, citizen development and operational efficiency as major drivers.

AI lowers the construction cost still further. An employee can increasingly assemble an internal application from APIs, existing SaaS platforms, generated code and automation tools without commissioning a traditional software project.

This probably reduces demand for custom development of **ordinary business applications**.

But it simultaneously increases the number of systems that must interact. Thoughtworks already warns of "AI-accelerated shadow IT": quickly generated applications and integrations that solve immediate problems while introducing security, data and maintainability concerns.

Custom development therefore shifts toward places where standard tools stop: differentiated workflows, complex integration, unusual domains, performance requirements, specialized user experiences, legacy environments, regulation and sophisticated engineering.

## 10. Open source becomes even more important—and more consequential

AI has not displaced open-source software. GitHub recorded **1.12 billion contributions to public and open-source repositories in 2025**, up 13% year over year, alongside record numbers of new contributors.

Open source remains the substrate on which most modern software is assembled. But that creates an increasingly important distinction between merely *using* open source and being capable of understanding, maintaining and governing it.

Software supply-chain risk has risen sharply. Sonatype says it identified more than **454,000 new malicious packages during 2025**, bringing its cumulative dataset above 1.2 million. Its data also shows package ecosystems operating at enormous scale, with trillions of annual downloads. Those figures come from a security vendor and should therefore be treated as its observed dataset rather than a census of all attacks, but the directional trend is difficult to dispute.

Open-source competence consequently becomes part of supply-chain competence. Suppliers that actively understand the ecosystems they use—and particularly organizations that contribute to or maintain important projects—can offer something increasingly meaningful to enterprise buyers: provenance, expertise and influence over critical technology rather than merely consumption of it.

## 11. Security and regulation are turning engineering discipline into a commercial requirement

This trend is particularly strong in Europe.

The EU Cyber Resilience Act changes cybersecurity from a best-practice discussion into a product-lifecycle obligation for many products containing software. Reporting obligations start on **September 11, 2026**, with the main requirements becoming applicable on **December 11, 2027**. Manufacturers must address cybersecurity during design and development, manage vulnerabilities during the support period and exercise due diligence over third-party—including open-source—components.

Enterprise procurement is already moving in this direction. ENISA's 2025 NIS investment study found 90% of organizations surveyed had implemented specific third-party or supply-chain security controls; common measures included requiring supplier security standards or certifications, performing supplier assessments and adding cybersecurity clauses to contracts.

This changes competition among development suppliers.

A provider's engineering process, dependency management, access controls, incident handling, software provenance and security practices increasingly become part of the product being purchased.

Security maturity therefore moves from back-office hygiene to **sales qualification and competitive differentiation**.

## 12. What becomes commoditized—and what becomes more valuable

Taken together, the research suggests a widening valuation gap.

The most exposed activities are repetitive implementation, boilerplate application development, straightforward migrations, simple test generation, generic web CRUD work and staff augmentation based primarily on technology keywords. Low-code platforms, AI coding agents, offshore suppliers, freelancers and internal developers can all compete for this work.

The stronger positions are different:

1. **Deep domain expertise** where understanding the problem is harder than writing the code.
2. **Architecture and systems integration**, particularly across complex legacy and modern environments.
3. **Specialized engineering**, including developer tooling, compilers, languages, embedded systems, data infrastructure and other technically demanding areas.
4. **Modernization**, where AI can accelerate understanding but responsibility for preserving behavior remains difficult.
5. **AI engineering with guardrails**, integrating models with business rules, tools, data, validation and human control.
6. **Platform and developer-experience engineering**, which multiplies the productivity of entire engineering organizations.
7. **Security-sensitive and regulated development**, where trust, traceability and lifecycle responsibility matter.
8. **Reusable intellectual property and open-source expertise**, which allows a supplier to deliver more value without linearly increasing headcount.

These categories share one property: **the scarce resource is not typing speed**.

It is judgment.

# Likely evolution through 2029

I would expect five developments to dominate the next few years.

### Development productivity will rise, but less spectacularly than code-generation benchmarks imply

AI will remove substantial amounts of implementation effort. However, a portion of that gain will be absorbed by increased software scope, additional verification and the natural tendency to build things that previously would not have justified their cost. DORA's findings and the opposing Google/METR productivity experiments make a universal "X% faster" forecast unjustifiable.

### Smaller, more senior teams will become economically attractive

The composition of teams should gradually move toward engineers who can understand systems, make decisions, orchestrate tools and review output. This does not mean junior developers disappear, but the traditional model in which junior implementation work finances their learning becomes harder to sustain.

### Customers will increasingly question hourly pricing

Not every engagement can become outcome-based, but AI productivity makes conversations about efficiency unavoidable. Companies that can confidently estimate and deliver fixed scopes will have more opportunity to retain productivity gains. Providers with reusable technology have an even stronger position.

### Specialist suppliers can gain relative advantage

Large service companies retain enormous advantages in procurement reach and delivery scale; ISG notes that large multinationals already capture a significant proportion of growing engineering-services contract value.  But AI also reduces the amount of manpower necessary to tackle substantial problems. That can increase the competitive reach of small teams possessing unusually deep expertise.

### The real battleground will be trusted automation

Generating a prototype will continue getting easier. Turning it into a trustworthy engineering system will remain difficult. Context, architecture, verification, tests, semantics, security, auditability and lifecycle management become the mechanism by which increasingly powerful AI is converted into enterprise value.

# Implications for the software development business model

The old services equation was roughly:

**Revenue = developers × utilization × daily rate.**

That equation will remain relevant for years, but it becomes strategically dangerous as the sole model because every improvement in engineering productivity threatens the first two variables.

A stronger long-term equation is closer to:

**Value = expertise × leverage × responsibility.**

Expertise means understanding something the customer cannot easily obtain elsewhere. Leverage comes from AI, open source, frameworks, automation, reusable IP and accumulated engineering knowledge. Responsibility means being able to solve the customer's actual problem rather than merely supplying implementation capacity.

That changes how a development company should think about growth. Growing headcount is no longer the only—or necessarily the best—way to grow its addressable project size. The alternative is to increase the amount of customer value a small expert team can deliver.

It also changes marketing. Technical competence alone becomes harder to differentiate when everyone can demonstrate impressive prototypes. Proof of understanding, recognized expertise, open-source authority, concrete engineering results, reference architectures and evidence that the supplier can safely solve difficult problems become more persuasive.

And it changes customer relationships. Deep, long-term partnerships become particularly valuable because **context is becoming an economic asset**. The better a development partner understands the customer's domain, architecture, constraints and organization, the more effectively both humans and AI can work. Replacing such a partner with commodity capacity destroys increasingly valuable context.

## Overall conclusion

The evidence does **not** point toward the end of the software development business.

It points toward the end of an unusually favorable period for **undifferentiated software development labor**.

Software demand continues to expand. Organizations are still struggling to find the right skills. Outsourcing budgets remain substantial. Engineering-services spending is growing. And AI is creating entirely new software categories.

But customers have more ways than ever to produce ordinary software: internal teams, global engineering centers, open source, SaaS, low-code platforms, freelancers and increasingly AI agents. The simple act of turning a specification into code therefore carries less scarcity value.

The businesses best positioned for the next phase will sell something harder to reproduce: **domain understanding, engineering judgment, architecture, reusable technology, integration capability and responsibility for getting difficult software right.**

AI then becomes a force multiplier rather than a threat to the business model.
